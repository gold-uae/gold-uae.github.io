How to Buy Gold in Dubai: A Step-by-Step Guide to Getting It Right

Buying gold in Dubai is simple once you know the sequence: rate, karat, weight, making charge, tax, receipt. The rate is public and identical everywhere, so your skill is spent on the other five. This guide walks through the process exactly as it happens at the counter, from the moment you open the live board on your phone to the moment you fold the invoice into your wallet, and then covers how the same rules apply when you buy gold online in the UAE.

It is written for a resident making a first serious purchase and for a visitor with a single afternoon, with examples drawn from Karama, Deira and the malls rather than from theory.

Quick answer

  • Check the live rate, pick a karat, then compare the making charge at three shops.
  • Insist on a receipt showing grams, karat, rate per gram, making charge and VAT.
  • Look for the purity stamp: 916 for 22K, 875 for 21K, 750 for 18K.
  • Online gold shopping in the UAE is safe with licensed retailers that show live pricing.
  • There is no wrong day to buy; there is only a wrong price for the making charge.

Step 1: Read the rate board before you read any price tag

The board at the shop entrance lists the per-gram rate for each karat. It is the Dubai Gold & Jewellery Group rate, revised during the day as the international ounce price moves, and it is the same whether you are in a Karama side street or on the ground floor of Dubai Mall. Two things to notice. First, the gap between karats is fixed by purity: 22K is 91.6% of the 24K figure, 21K is 87.5%, 18K is 75%. If a shop's board shows a 22K rate that is not roughly that fraction of its 24K rate, something is off. Second, the rate on the board is for pure metal weight; it never includes making or VAT, so no price tag in the shop should ever be as low as the board number multiplied by the grams.

Write the 22K and 21K figures down. From this moment you will be comparing everything to those two numbers.

Step 2: Choose the karat for the job

Karat is not a quality grade; it is a purity grade, and each one suits a purpose.

  • 24K (999) is pure gold: soft, deep yellow, used for bars, coins and some Chinese and Far Eastern jewellery. It bends and scratches, so it is rare in daily-wear pieces here.
  • 22K (916) is the Indian, Pakistani, Sri Lankan and Bangladeshi standard. If the piece will be worn at a wedding in Kochi or sold in Lahore, this is what buyers there expect.
  • 21K (875) is the Gulf, Egyptian and Levantine favourite: slightly harder, slightly cheaper per gram, and the most common karat on Emirati and Arab counters.
  • 18K (750) is for diamond-set, white and rose gold pieces, and for European-style fine jewellery.

A practical rule: match the karat to the market where the piece will eventually be sold or inherited. The karat guide goes deeper, and the 22K and 21K pages show each rate on its own. Do not let a salesperson steer you from 22K to 18K "because it is stronger" unless you actually want a diamond-set design; the lower karat simply means less gold for the same weight.

Step 3: Weigh, stamp, and separate the stones

When a piece catches your eye, ask for it to be weighed in front of you. Every licensed shop keeps a calibrated digital scale on the counter, and the number it shows is the number that must appear on the receipt. Take a 12-gram 22K chain as the example for this page: 12.00 g on the scale means 12.00 g on the invoice, not 12.5 rounded up for convenience.

Next find the purity stamp, usually inside a clasp, on the back of a pendant or inside a bangle. It reads 916, 875 or 750, sometimes with the shop's own mark. Dubai's hallmarking is strict and fake gold is rare in licensed shops, but reading the stamp takes five seconds and settles the karat question for good. The hallmark and purity test page shows photos of what to look for.

If the piece has stones, the gross weight includes them. Ask for the stone weight to be deducted so you pay the gold rate only on gold, with the stones priced separately on their own line. For cubic zirconia and small coloured stones, the fair deduction is usually the full stone weight; for diamonds, the shop will price the stones on carat and quality instead.

Step 4: Negotiate the making charge, not the gold

This is the step that decides whether you overpay. The making charge is the shop's fee for the craftsmanship, quoted either as dirhams per gram or as a percentage of the gold value. For a plain machine-made chain in a souk, the per-gram fee is modest; for a hand-finished bridal set it can be a large fraction of the metal value.

  1. Ask: "What is the making charge per gram?" If they give a percentage, convert it: multiply the board rate by the percentage to get the per-gram equivalent.
  2. Ask the same question at two more shops for a comparable piece. In Deira or Meena Bazaar the shops are metres apart; in a mall, walk the length of the gold floor.
  3. Return to your favourite and quote the lowest competing figure. Souk shops expect this; branded chains have less room but often waive part of the charge during promotions.
  4. Agree the per-gram making charge and ask for it to be written on the invoice as a separate line.

The making charges guide breaks down what is typical for each type of piece and explains what "zero making charge" offers really cost.

Carry a note of the weight and making charge of the best piece you have seen so far. By the third shop, the negotiation often goes faster because the salesperson realises you already have a benchmark.

Step 5: Understand the VAT line and the tourist refund

Jewellery in the UAE carries 5% VAT on the whole invoice, metal plus making charge. It is shown as a separate line and the shop's tax registration number must be printed on the receipt. Investment bars and coins of 99% purity or higher are zero-rated, which is one reason the bar market is priced so differently from the jewellery counter.

If you are a tourist, you can recover the VAT on jewellery at departure. The shop must be registered with the Planet tax-free scheme, the invoice must be at least AED 250, and the goods must leave the country within 90 days. The shop issues a tax-free tag at the time of purchase, and you validate it at the airport before check-in. The VAT and tax refund page gives the full procedure, including what happens when you have already checked the bag.

Residents cannot claim the refund, but they do benefit from the absence of import duty and from the competition that keeps making charges low. The net effect is that a resident's total price is still usually below what the same piece costs in most home countries.

Step 6: Pay safely and check the receipt before you walk out

Cards are accepted nearly everywhere, bank transfer is standard above a few thousand dirhams, and cash is still welcome in the souks. Some small shops offer a slightly lower making charge for cash because card fees cost them a percentage; others add a small surcharge for cards. Ask before you commit to a figure, not after.

Before leaving, read the invoice line by line. It should show the date, the karat with its purity number, net gold weight in grams to two decimals, the rate per gram applied, the making charge as a separate line, any stone charge, 5% VAT, the total, and the shop's name, address and tax number. Many invoices also carry a buy-back or exchange sentence; a common policy is full metal value against a new purchase, with a deduction for cash buy-back.

Keep the invoice with the piece. It is what you will show when you sell, exchange, insure or carry the gold through customs. The selling guide explains how much a good receipt is worth on the day you part with the piece.

Online gold shopping in the UAE: how the rules change

Buying gold online in the UAE has become routine. Branded jewellers run their own web stores, bullion dealers sell bars and coins with next-day delivery, and the major marketplaces list gold from licensed sellers. The economics are the same as in a shop, with three differences.

Choose sellers that show a live per-gram price on the product page, state the karat and net gold weight, and list a physical UAE address and tax number. Prefer pieces that arrive with a printed invoice identical to what a shop would give, because that invoice is your proof when you sell. For bars and coins, serial-numbered sealed packaging from a recognised refiner matters more than the retailer's brand; the coins guide explains why.

Gold: buy or sell today? Reading the rate without a crystal ball

The question people search most is whether today is a good day to buy or sell. Nobody can answer that reliably, and this site does not offer forecasts. What you can do is remove the guesswork that is removable.

Look at the price history to see where today sits within the last month and the last year. If the rate is near the top of its recent range, some buyers wait for a dip; if it is near the bottom, some sellers hold. Neither is a prediction, just a way of avoiding the extremes. Residents who buy for savings often split a purchase into monthly instalments, which averages the rate automatically.

Timing matters more for making charges than for the metal. Festival promotions before Eid, Diwali and during the Dubai Shopping Festival reduce the labour cost; quiet summer weekdays let you negotiate it. If you buy for wearing and gifting, pick the moment when the making charge is lowest and let the metal rate be what it is. If you buy for investment, read the price analysis page for the factors that historically move the market, and remember that over a long holding period the day of purchase matters far less than the premium you paid over spot.

Questions people ask

What is the best place to buy gold in Dubai for a first-timer?

For a first purchase, the Deira Gold Souk or Meena Bazaar in Bur Dubai are ideal because dozens of shops sit within a few minutes' walk, so comparing making charges is effortless and prices are kept honest by competition. If you prefer a fixed-price environment with a certificate and a clear exchange policy, a branded jeweller in a mall is a comfortable alternative, usually with slightly higher making charges.

Do I need an Emirates ID or passport to buy gold?

Small jewellery purchases need no documents. For larger transactions, shops are required by anti-money-laundering rules to record the buyer's identity, so expect to show an Emirates ID or passport and possibly to pay by transfer rather than cash above a threshold. Tourists wanting the VAT refund must show their passport at the time of purchase so the tax-free tag can be issued in their name.

Is it safe to buy gold online in the UAE?

Yes, provided the seller is a licensed UAE retailer or bullion dealer with a physical address, a tax registration number and a live per-gram price on the listing. Insist on a detailed invoice, insured delivery with signature, and a returns window. For bars and coins, buy sealed, serial-numbered products from recognised refiners. Avoid any seller that quotes a flat price without stating the karat and the net gold weight.

Can I return or exchange gold jewellery after buying it?

Most shops will not refund cash on a whim, but almost all will exchange a piece against another purchase at full metal value, often within a stated period and sometimes with the original making charge credited. The policy is printed on the receipt; read it before paying. Branded chains tend to have more formal exchange windows, while souk shops handle it case by case, which is one more reason to keep the invoice.

How do I know the shop's rate is correct?

Compare the rate on the invoice with the live rate shown on this site at the time of purchase. They should match to within the intraday movement. The per-gram rate for your karat should also be the right fraction of the 24K rate: 91.6% for 22K, 87.5% for 21K and 75% for 18K. If the shop applies a higher per-gram figure and calls it the rate, it is quietly folding a making charge into the metal.
